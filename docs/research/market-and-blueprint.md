# Building the Football Manager of the NFL

A realistic NFL management sim represents one of the clearest unmet demands in sports gaming — a gap that has persisted for over a decade. **Football Manager proves that millions of players will pay premium prices for a pure management simulation** with no action gameplay whatsoever, generating an estimated $100M+ annually for Sega. Yet the NFL equivalent simply does not exist at quality. Every current American football management sim is built by a one-to-three person team, earns under $100K lifetime on Steam, and reaches only hundreds of concurrent players. Meanwhile, **Madden's franchise mode — the closest mainstream offering — is so neglected that #FixMaddenFranchise trended nationally in 2020**, and EA continues to prioritize Ultimate Team microtransactions over management depth. For a solo developer willing to commit 10–15 months to an MVP, the opportunity is real: a well-executed NFL management sim targeting frustrated Madden franchise players and FM-curious NFL fans could realistically generate $75K–$825K in its first two years, with the potential for much more if it captures even a fraction of the audience that clearly wants this game to exist.

## Football Manager's formula: a world, not a game

Football Manager's dominance begins with a design philosophy articulated by SI director Miles Jacobson: "We don't make a game, we make a world. We make a world of football — do anything you want in that world." This distinction matters. FM doesn't simulate matches and call it a day. It simulates an entire footballing ecosystem — **800,000+ real players across 118 leagues**, with club finances, board expectations, fan sentiment, media dynamics, and coaching hierarchies all running simultaneously in the background.

The core loop is deceptively simple: manage tactics, scout players, negotiate transfers, develop youth, and compete. But the depth within each system creates emergent storytelling that no scripted game can match. A 15-year-old academy product you nurtured through training and loan spells becomes a World Cup winner a decade later. Your underdog club reaches the Champions League final against the odds. These aren't cutscenes — they're organic outcomes of interlocking simulation systems. Individual players routinely log **1,000–2,000+ hours per annual release**, and FM24 reached **19 million players** across all platforms, making it the most-played entry in the series' 30-year history.

The secret weapon behind FM's realism is its **volunteer scouting network of approximately 1,300–1,400 real-world researchers**, organized hierarchically under about 100 head researchers who each oversee a nation or league. These unpaid scouts watch actual matches and rate players across **40+ visible attributes on a 1–20 scale**, with an additional 230+ hidden attributes governing in-game behavior. The database is so accurate that professional football clubs — including Everton FC, which signed a formal data-sharing agreement with SI — use it for actual scouting. As SI's Tom Markham noted: "Manchester City, with their four clubs, have 40 scouts globally. That really shows the depth of the network."

Technically, the match engine operates as an **agent-based simulation** where each of 22 players makes autonomous decisions every quarter-second based on their attributes, tactical instructions, morale, fatigue, and contextual awareness. The visual 3D representation is a secondary rendering layer — the simulation runs independently beneath it. FM26 marked a historic transition to the **Unity engine** for graphics and UI, while retaining SI's proprietary systems for the match engine, tactical AI, and player decision-making. This hybrid approach — Unity for presentation, custom code for simulation — is instructive for any developer considering this space.

FM's monetization is notably clean. The PC version sells for **$59.99** with no microtransactions (a principle Jacobson has defended vocally against Sega pressure). The only paid add-on is an in-game editor for roughly $7. FM Mobile is distributed through Netflix Games as part of a subscription bundle. The franchise has sold **33+ million copies cumulatively** as of 2021, and the community's modding ecosystem — face packs with 540,000+ portraits, logo packs, real-name fixes, custom databases, tactical exports — extends the game's lifespan far beyond what SI could produce alone. Community sites like SortitoutSI, FM Scout, and FM-Base function as de facto extensions of the development team.

## The NFL sim market is vast in demand but barren in supply

The landscape of existing NFL management sims is sparse, underfunded, and reaching only tiny audiences — despite overwhelming evidence that a large market exists.

**Front Office Football Nine** (FOF9), developed solo by Jim Gindin since 1998, is the deepest option available. Priced at $29.99 on Steam with **162 reviews and roughly $91,000 in estimated lifetime revenue**, it offers text-based play-by-play simulation, full GM control, franchise relocation, and a 25-year pedigree. But it averages just **22 concurrent players on Steam** and remains Windows-only with a deliberately austere interface. **Draft Day Sports: Pro Football 2026** by Wolverine Studios ($24.99) adds custom play creation and a college-to-pro pipeline, but carries mixed Steam reviews (46% positive for the 2025 edition) and an even smaller community. **Football GM** by ZenGM is entirely free, browser-based, and surprisingly capable for roster management — but its simplified NFL rules and bare-bones presentation limit its appeal. **Football Mogul** has iterated annually since 1999 but isn't even on Steam, severely limiting discoverability. **Progression Football**, a free web-based multiplayer sim, earned rave reviews from those who found it ("best simulation football game I have ever played") but serves only single-digit concurrent users.

The total active player count across all NFL management sims combined likely numbers in the low thousands. Compare this to the potential audience: **NFL is America's most-watched sport with 200M+ fans**, Madden sells 5–10 million copies annually (with an estimated 30–40% playing franchise mode), and the "FM for NFL" request has appeared on Reddit, Steam forums, and sports gaming communities continuously for over a decade. A 2011 Sports Interactive community thread asking "Will There Ever Be A Football Manager NFL American Football?" generated discussion for 14 years with no satisfactory answer.

The failure of Madden's franchise mode amplifies this gap. Operation Sports, the leading sports gaming publication, has published years of critical coverage: "Madden's franchise mode has no clear direction," "the mode feels tired and lifeless," and franchise loyalists "have yet again been thrown a proverbial bone." EA's own forums overflow with complaints that franchise mode was better in Madden 06 than in games released a decade later. The revenue incentive structure is the culprit — **Madden Ultimate Team generates billions in microtransaction revenue**, making deep franchise features a low-priority investment. This structural neglect creates a permanent opening for a dedicated management sim.

## Designing the systems: what an NFL management sim must get right

American football is uniquely well-suited to discrete event simulation. Unlike soccer's continuous flow, **every NFL play is a self-contained event** with a clear start, resolution, and outcome — making play-by-play simulation the natural architecture. A game of roughly 120–150 plays, each involving 22 players making position-specific decisions, yields approximately 1,540 player-events per game and about 419,000 per regular season. This is computationally trivial compared to FM's simulation of hundreds of leagues with thousands of concurrent matches.

The match engine should resolve each play through layered probability calculations. For a pass play: offensive line pass-blocking versus defensive line pass-rush determines pocket time; route-running versus coverage skill determines receiver separation; quarterback accuracy (split into short, medium, and deep) combined with pressure modifiers and defender ball skills determines completion probability; and receiver speed/agility versus tackler pursuit determines yards after catch. Run plays follow a similar chain: blocking scheme assessment, hole quality, second-level evasion, and breakaway probability. Each variable draws from player attributes weighted by scheme matchup and situational context (down, distance, score, fatigue, weather). The key insight from FM's engine is that outcomes should emerge from individual player interactions rather than being predetermined — and that stochastic variance must be bounded so that superior teams win more often without making upsets impossible.

The NFL's financial architecture demands the deepest simulation systems. The **salary cap ($301.2 million for 2026)** is a hard constraint requiring players to understand signing bonus proration across up to five years, dead money acceleration on cuts (Tua Tagovailoa's release created a record **$99.2 million dead cap hit**), void years used to spread cap charges (the Eagles carry $427M+ in void-year commitments through 2033), contract restructures converting base salary to prorated bonus, and the distinction between likely-to-be-earned and not-likely-to-be-earned incentives. This system alone — if faithfully simulated — would differentiate the game from every existing competitor.

The draft and scouting pipeline requires procedural generation of 300–400 college prospects annually, with quality distributed realistically: roughly 5–8 first-round talents, 15–20 Day 2 contributors, and a long tail of late-round lottery tickets and undrafted free agents. Scouting accuracy should use confidence intervals that narrow with investment — combine data reveals physical measurables precisely, interviews reveal character traits imperfectly, and college film provides production data colored by conference strength. The game must include "boom or bust" prospects with wide potential ranges to create authentic draft uncertainty. For the attribute system, a **1–20 scale (FM-style) rather than Madden's 1–99** avoids rating inflation and provides meaningful differentiation, with position-specific technical attributes layered over universal physical and mental ratings.

Player development curves vary dramatically by position and should not follow smooth mathematical functions. Research shows NFL careers more closely resemble **plateaus with sudden drop-offs** rather than gradual declines — a player performs at roughly consistent levels then suddenly falls off, often triggered by injury. Running backs peak earliest (24–26) and decline fastest, quarterbacks can sustain peak performance from 28–33, and offensive linemen age most gracefully with decline onset around 32–34. Physical attributes (speed, agility) should degrade faster than mental ones (awareness, decision-making).

Licensing is the elephant in the room. **EA holds exclusive NFL simulation game rights through 2030**, covering team names, logos, uniforms, and stadiums. The NFLPA license for player names and likenesses is separate, with EA's current deal running through February 2027. However, the proven workaround is straightforward: ship with fictional teams and players, design robust modding tools, and let the community create real-name roster files within days of release. This is exactly how Front Office Football, Football Mogul, and Football GM have operated legally for decades. FM itself uses this approach for unlicensed leagues like the Bundesliga, with community "Real Name Fix" mods restoring accurate data. The game should include an open, documented file format (XML or JSON) for all team and player data, a pre-game editor tool, and ideally Steam Workshop integration for one-click mod installation.

## The solo developer's toolkit: Godot, SQLite, and radical scoping

For a simulation-heavy, UI-driven game with no need for 3D physics or high-fidelity graphics, **Godot Engine is the strongest choice**. It is completely free and open-source under the MIT license with zero royalties at any revenue level — eliminating the licensing anxiety that Unity's 2023 pricing controversy created. Godot's built-in UI system was designed specifically for complex interface layouts (the Godot editor itself is built with it), its dedicated 2D engine avoids the overhead of rendering pipelines designed for 3D games, and **GDScript is a Python-inspired language deliberately optimized for accessibility** by developers with limited programming backgrounds. Godot exports to Windows, Mac, Linux, Android, iOS, and HTML5 from a single codebase, and its Custom Resources system enables clean separation of game data from logic — essential for a sports sim managing thousands of player records. At the 2024 GMTK Game Jam, **37% of entries used Godot**, reflecting explosive community momentum and growing tutorial availability.

The secondary option for a developer with web experience is a React/TypeScript stack wrapped with Tauri (desktop) and Capacitor (mobile). This approach leverages the massive ecosystem of web UI libraries that excel at exactly the kind of data tables, forms, and responsive layouts a management sim requires. Browser-based sports sims like Hattrick have sustained hundreds of thousands of users with this model. However, for a single-player game targeting Steam distribution, a game engine provides a more conventional and better-supported path.

For data storage, **SQLite is the clear choice**: embedded, zero-configuration, serverless, and capable of handling FM-scale databases of 800K+ records with ease. A save file can be a single .sqlite file that's trivially backed up, shared, or version-controlled. SQL queries make complex lookups natural — "show me all free agent quarterbacks under 30 with an accuracy rating above 15" — and the cross-platform compatibility is perfect. The recommended architecture layers SQLite for persistent storage beneath in-memory data structures for active simulation, with JSON files for moddable configuration data (rules, league structure, settings).

The most critical decision for a solo developer is ruthless MVP scoping. The minimum viable product should include a single 17-game season with playoffs for 32 teams, basic player attributes (5–8 key stats per position rather than 30+), a text-based probabilistic match engine with no visual representation, roster management with depth charts, a simplified draft (perhaps 3–4 rounds), basic free agency, a simple salary cap without restructures or dead money, rule-based AI opponents, and save/load functionality. Everything else — coaching staff, scouting systems, player development, trading, match visualization, mobile ports, modding tools, multi-season careers — gets added iteratively after the core loop proves engaging.

Historical precedent supports this approach. Championship Manager was created by Paul and Oliver Collyer, **two teenage brothers coding on a BBC Micro starting in 1983**, who spent six years of intermittent bedroom development before the 1992 commercial release. That first version was pure text. Motorsport Manager started as a mobile game built by a single developer, Christian West, who quit his job and coded it in his bedroom — the mobile success attracted Sega as a publisher for the expanded PC version. Out of the Park Baseball was primarily the work of German programmer Markus Heinsohn and eventually scored **96/100 on Metacritic**, higher than The Orange Box. Front Office Football has been sustained by Jim Gindin alone for 25+ years. The pattern is universal: start extremely simple, iterate over years, and let depth accumulate through sustained commitment rather than attempting a feature-complete launch.

A realistic timeline for the MVP is **10–15 months working part-time (15–20 hours per week)** or 6–9 months full-time: 2–3 months learning Godot and prototyping, 4–6 months building core systems to alpha, and 3–4 months polishing through beta to Early Access launch. The architecture should follow a Model-View-Controller pattern (not Entity Component System, which is designed for real-time action games), with a global event bus for communication between systems, utility-based AI for opponent decision-making, and a seeded random number generator enabling reproducible simulations for debugging and replay sharing.

## From prototype to revenue: a staged business model

The recommended monetization strategy is a **premium one-time purchase at $19.99 during Early Access, rising to $24.99 at 1.0 release**, with an optional in-game editor DLC at $4.99–$6.99 following FM's proven model. This price point sits below FOF9 ($29.99) and OOTP ($39.99) while reflecting the solo developer's brand-new entry status. Free-to-play is explicitly wrong for this audience — management sim players are the demographic most hostile to microtransactions, as demonstrated by the backlash when FM26's console version introduced in-app purchases. Subscription models have no precedent in the genre. Annual releases should be avoided initially; the exhausting pace of annual development is unsustainable for a solo creator and leads to the discounting spiral visible in Draft Day Sports' history.

The go-to-market strategy should follow a staged pipeline proven by successful indie games. **Start on itch.io** with a free or pay-what-you-want prototype to validate interest and gather feedback with zero financial risk (versus Steam's $100 registration fee and higher-stakes review environment). Build a Discord community from day one, organized with channels for bug reports, feature suggestions, and general discussion — as a solo developer, personal engagement with every community member is both feasible and powerful. Post weekly devlogs on itch.io, Reddit (targeting r/Madden, r/nfl, r/indiegaming, r/gamedev), and Twitter/X. Set up a Patreon with tiers offering early build access and feature voting rights — realistic expectation is $200–$1,000 per month during development.

Before Steam launch, create a "Coming Soon" page and accumulate wishlists aggressively. **Steam's algorithm rewards launch momentum**, so 2,000–5,000 wishlists at Early Access launch dramatically improves visibility. Submit a demo to Steam Next Fest for free exposure. At launch, seed review copies to YouTube creators and streamers covering NFL content, Madden franchise mode, and sports management games.

Revenue projections based on comparable games suggest a realistic range:

- **Conservative (5,000 copies)**: ~$70,000 net after Steam's 30% cut
- **Moderate (15,000 copies)**: ~$210,000 net
- **Optimistic (50,000 copies)**: ~$770,000 net

For context, FOF9's estimated lifetime Steam revenue of $91,000 represents the floor for a text-based NFL sim with minimal marketing from a developer who has been in the space for 25 years. A new entrant with a modern UI, cross-platform availability, active community engagement, and smart positioning as "the management sim Madden refuses to be" has structural advantages that FOF never pursued.

A mobile port at $6.99–$9.99 should follow after the desktop version stabilizes. Motorsport Manager demonstrated that mobile can sustain a studio when PC sequels aren't viable — and the management sim format translates to touchscreens better than most genres, provided the UI is redesigned rather than simply scaled down. The practical approach is building the core simulation as platform-agnostic logic, then creating separate UI layers for desktop (multi-panel layouts) and mobile (tabbed navigation with drill-down detail views).

## The competitive moat is execution and community, not features

The fundamental insight from this research is that the barrier to success in this space is not technical complexity, licensing, or even the feature list — it's sustained execution quality and community cultivation. Every existing NFL management sim was built by one to three people. The technology required is well within a solo developer's reach. The licensing obstacle is solved by the same fictional-names-plus-community-mods approach that FM has used for decades. The audience exists, is vocal about its desires, and has been waiting for over a decade.

What no existing competitor has delivered is the combination that FM perfected for soccer: **modern, polished UI plus deep simulation plus accessible onboarding plus cross-platform availability plus a thriving modding community**. Front Office Football has depth but no visual appeal. Draft Day Sports has ambition but mixed execution. Football GM has accessibility but simplified mechanics. Madden has the license but treats franchise players as an afterthought. The gap is not in any single feature — it's in the holistic package.

The smartest strategic move available to a solo developer entering this space is to treat the post-2030 NFL licensing landscape as a long-term option. EA's exclusive simulation deal runs through 2030. A game that launches in 2027–2028 with fictional teams and community mods, builds a loyal audience of 10,000–50,000 players over two to three years, and then pursues official NFL licensing when the exclusive deal expires would be positioned to capture a market that has been artificially suppressed for over two decades. Football Manager itself didn't start with Premier League licensing — it earned it through decades of quality. The same path is available here, and the destination may be worth billions in a sport that dwarfs European soccer within the American market.
